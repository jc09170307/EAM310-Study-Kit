1 · Framework: 聞診 (Wén Zhén) — The Second Pillar

Wén Zhén is the second of the Four Diagnostic Methods (望聞問切) and has two distinct components that share one character (聞 — "to perceive with the ear/nose").

ComponentCovers
HearingVoice, breathing, cough, vomiting, hiccup, borborygmi, sighing, belching — any sound emitted by the patient
SmellingBody odor, and odor of bodily secretions (breath, sweat, sputum, urine/stools, vaginal discharge, intestinal gas)
Quick Recall: 聞診 = Hearing + Smelling, the 2nd of the Four Pillars (望聞問切).
2 · Voice & Zang-Fu Relationship

Three organ systems govern distinct aspects of the voice.

OrganRoleGoverns
LungGateway of the voice; movement of qi helps create soundsProduction of sound
KidneyRoot of the voice; root of yuan qi, controls reception of qiVolume of the voice
Spleen & StomachAcquired root of qi; influences vocalizationStrength of the voice
Quick Recall: Lung=production, Kidney=volume, Spleen/Stomach=strength.

Quick Recall: Loud/excessive=Full/Heat. Weak/reluctant=Empty/Cold. Sudden loss=Wind-Heat invasion; gradual loss=Lung-Qi/Yin deficiency.
Abnormal Vocalizations: Hoarseness (by onset)
Onset / QualityPattern
Sudden onset, low pitch, raspyExterior syndrome
Chronic, gradual intensityBlood stagnation
Hoarseness with plum-pit sensation & coughPhlegm-Heat

Heads Up — "Worse in the evening" is the differentiator that flags Yin deficiency (dusk/night aggravation is the classic Yin-deficiency-heat timing pattern) — don't just memorize "chronic," since Blood stagnation is also chronic but lacks the evening-worsening detail.

Panting (severe respiratory distress)

Rapid, irregular respiration, extreme to the point that the nostrils move, with uneven entrance and exit of breath, opening of the mouth, raising of the shoulders, and difficulty lying down.

6 · Vomiting, Belching & Hiccup

All three share the same root mechanism — Rebellious Stomach Qi — and differ only in what is expelled and how.

SymptomCommon RootDifferences
VomitingRebellious ST QiFood, fluid or sputum flows up from the Stomach and out of the mouth with sound
BelchingAir or gas flows up from the stomach and makes sound passing through the throat
HiccupST Qi's rebellion causes involuntary spasms of the diaphragm and makes a short sound
SymptomDeficiency/Cold PresentationExcess/Heat Presentation
VomitingComes slowly, sound of retching relatively low, faint, and without force — frequently ST deficiency and ColdComes relatively suddenly, sound of retching relatively loud and forceful — frequently ST-Heat
BelchingShort and low sound — ST-Qi deficiency, or ST-Qi deficiency with ColdLoud and long sound — retention of food, ST-Heat, or LV-Qi invading the Stomach
HiccupQuiet, low sound — often ST-Qi or ST-Yin deficiencyLoud sound — often LV-Qi invading the Stomach
Quick Recall: All three = Rebellious ST Qi. Loud/forceful=Excess-Heat or LV invading ST. Quiet/faint=ST deficiency (Qi, Yin, or Cold).

8 · Five Element Sounds — 五音 Wǔ Yīn

Voice quality/expression, paired to the Five Elements. Distinct topic from the Five (Element) Odors in Section 11 — don't conflate the two lists.

ElementSoundPinyin / HanziCharacter of the Sound
WoodShoutingHū 呼Gives forcefulness to the voice; an indication of assertion. When one shouts they want to be heard and/or are asking, explicitly or implicitly, for changes to be made. Frequently becomes louder at moments when the person is not really needing to be particularly assertive.
FireLaughingXiào 笑Occurs when we express moments of joy. Does not necessarily mean an actual laugh (can include a "pre-laugh" without one emerging, or there could be one). Fire is a very yang Element, so just as laughter seems to rise upwards and outwards, so does this sound.
EarthSingingChàng 唱May occur naturally when singing a lullaby with a child in arms, or soothing an anxious or distressed animal/person. Has increased variation in pitch — the voice goes up and down more frequently.
MetalWeeping— (no pinyin/hanzi given on slide)Consumes or dissolves qi. More of a non-verbal sound but based on an expressive, emotional quality. When speaking with a weep the person might easily begin to cry; other times there could be a choking back or a faltering in the words.
WaterGroaning— (no pinyin/hanzi given on slide)Has little movement or modulation in its quality. Is one of flatness, as if the more normal ups and downs of the voice have been squeezed or flattened out.
Quick Recall: Shout=Wood, Laugh=Fire, Sing=Earth, Weep=Metal, Groan=Water — same order as the standard Five Element cycle.

11 · The Five (Named) Odors
Heads Up — Unlike the Five Element Sounds (Section 8), these five odor-description slides do NOT explicitly label each smell with a Zang-Fu element on the lecture slides. Also, the "Scorched" slide title uses the character xiào 笑 — the same hanzi as "Laughing" from Section 8. Classically the Fire-element scorched odor is jiāo 焦, a different character. This looks like a copy/paste label on the lecture slide (already flagged on the Prep Sheet) — confirm with Prof. Schrier rather than memorizing 笑 for "scorched," and don't assume the classical Wood/Fire/Earth/Metal/Water odor pairing (sour–scorched–fragrant–rank–putrid) maps 1:1 onto this list without confirming it in class.
OdorSensory Description
Rancid (Sōu 餿)Associated with fat that is no longer fresh or slightly sour. Also related to the smell of newly-mown grass, but not pleasant. Effect on the inside of the nose is somewhat prickling and makes one wrinkle their nose.
ScorchedResembles what someone would describe as the smell in life — e.g. burnt toast, clothes coming out of the tumble dryer, a shirt just scorched while ironing.
Fragrant (Xiāng 香)Often thought of as a pleasing smell such as flowers, but this odor is the opposite and not as pleasing — more associated with a cloying, sickly sweet smell with a tendency to linger in the nostrils.
Rotten (Làn 爛)Fills the inside of one's nose with tiny prickles and may make one clench the inside of their nose. Rotting meat is the best verbal description, or a trash can/garbage truck where many substances are decomposing.
Putrid (Fǔ 腐)Described as the smell of rotting meat. Can describe the smell of water in a stagnant pond or the smell of stale urine. Has a sharp and aggressive quality, making one's nose clench up.

14 · Clinical Case — Apply It
Mr. Alvarez, 38 years old — presents with a 3-day history of a loud, productive cough with thick yellow sputum that has a strong, rotten smell. His voice is loud and he speaks rapidly, without reluctance. He reports being thirsty, prefers cold drinks, and his stool has been drier than usual. Breath odor is strong and foul.

Working through it: Loud + productive cough with sound → Full pattern (Section 5). Strong, rotten-smelling sputum → Heat in the Lungs, most likely Phlegm-Heat (Section 12, Sputum). Loud voice, no reluctance to talk → consistent with Full/Heat (Section 3). Strong, foul breath → Stomach Heat or food retention (Section 12, Breath) — worth asking about diet to differentiate from a purely Lung-origin heat. Preference for cold drinks + drier stool supports an overall Heat picture.

Working TCM pattern: Phlegm-Heat in the Lung (with a possible Stomach-Heat component to investigate further via Inquiry).

If untreated and this Excess-Heat pattern began to consume Yin over time, expect the picture to shift toward Yin-deficiency signs: voice could become hoarse and worse in the evening (Section 3), cough could dry out (Section 5), sweat could become scantier but still concerning if putrid-smelling (Section 12), and breath odor could shift from strong/foul toward drier without necessarily resolving.
